How This App Helps the User

If you're an IT professional adopting AI, the hard part isn't getting answers from it. The hard part is deciding what to ask, what to trust, and when to stop. This app gives you a safe place to practise those decisions, and shows you the evidence of how you made them.

1. The Short Answer

The app doesn't ask what you know about AI. It puts you in a situation where you have to run the work yourself: set a goal, pick a tool, test it, read the failures, question the advice, and say honestly what's still broken.

Your final score matters less than how you got there. Two people can submit the same design, and the one who checked their work and named its limits gets the better profile.

It rewards judgment

A cheap design that works beats an expensive one. Bigger isn't automatically better.

It rewards checking

The app gives you a flawed AI tip and wrong test labels on purpose. Only checking catches them.

It rewards honesty

Some failures can't be tuned away. Naming them is the right answer.

2. What These Terms Mean Here

These are working definitions for this document, not industry standards.

Agentic mindset: working the way a good agent works. You hold a goal, choose tools, act, observe the result, correct course, and know when to stop or escalate. You stay responsible for the outcome, even when AI did the work.

AI amplifier: someone whose output grows with AI because their judgment directs it. AI multiplies what you bring. Clear goals and good checks get multiplied, and so do vague goals and blind trust.

AI as a shortcutAI as an amplifier
Asks for the answerStates the goal, then asks for options and trade-offs
Accepts output that sounds rightTests output against a measure
Reaches for the biggest toolFinds the cheapest tool that's good enough
Retries when something failsFinds out why it failed, then changes one thing
Reports only what workedReports what works and what doesn't

5. Step-by-Step: Where Each Habit Is Tested

The numbers below come from running this path through the app's scoring code.

Step 1: Start cheap
Habit: right-sized tool

Keyword search alone scores 0.42 for 136 cost units. Now you have a baseline, so every later change has to earn its cost.

Step 2: Test the AI's advice
Habit: verify AI advice

The app's AI tip sounds confident: set the minimum similarity to 0.8. Follow it, and the score collapses to 0.13. Someone who trusts the tip submits a broken design. Someone who tests it sees the drop and rejects it.

Step 3: Change what the evidence points to
Habit: diagnose before changing

Reading the failures points to long reviews burying key facts and duplicates filling the top five. Sentence chunks and duplicate removal lift the score to 0.56, with no bigger model involved.

Step 4: Doubt the test itself
Habit: question the measure

Three of the visible test labels are wrong on purpose. If you tune to them, you're chasing bad data. A metric is only as good as its labels, and this is true of any AI evaluation.

Step 5: Accept what can't be fixed
Habit: own the outcome

Sarcasm, negation and comparison direction still fail. Even the larger MiniLM model scored no better on comparisons (0.64 against 0.69). The right move is to stop tuning and report the limit clearly.

Step 6: Face queries you haven't seen
Habit: pick the right-sized tool

Your design is tested on 30 held-out queries. This path scores 0.54. The best known design scores 0.59, but needs MiniLM, which costs about ten times as much per token. Being close at a fraction of the cost counts as the stronger choice.

8. Getting the Most From It

  1. Try it without any answers first. Your first attempt shows your real habits. A walkthrough only shows you someone else's.
  2. Change one thing per run. If you change three settings, you won't know which one helped.
  3. Give your assistant real evidence. Paste in your failed queries and scores, rather than asking in general terms.
  4. Be strict in your self-review. Half the profile depends on it, so generous ticks only hide what you need to practise.
  5. Retry and compare. Your past attempts stay on your device, so you can see what changed.
  6. Use one habit at work this week. Before you accept the next AI suggestion, write down how you'd know if it's wrong.

Can Python Exec Builder make a file that phones can open?

No, and this app doesn't need it. There are three separate reasons:

  • Phones can't run an exe. An exe is a Windows program. Python Exec Builder's own guide says its exe works only on 64-bit Windows, and that a phone app is a different kind of package.
  • The builder needs a Flask app. It packages Python Flask apps with PyInstaller, which doesn't support Android. This app is plain HTML and JavaScript, with no Python to package.
  • A link already does the job. Tapping a link is the "click and open in the browser" step. The builder's guide gives the same advice for phones: host it, don't package it.

10. What the App Doesn't Test Yet

The app tests the thinking behind agentic work. It doesn't yet test building or supervising a real agent.

  • No real agents. You run the loop yourself. Nothing plans, calls tools or acts on its own, so you don't practise delegating to an agent, setting its permissions, or reviewing its multi-step work.
  • AI use is self-reported. The app can't see your assistant in the other tab. It only knows what you log, so prompt quality isn't measured.
  • The traps are limited. Once you know them, a repeat attempt tests memory more than judgment. There's only one dataset and one challenge so far.
  • It's one narrow task. Search is a good setting for judgment, but it doesn't cover coding, automation or incident work.
  • The benefit isn't proven. No pilot has run yet, so nobody has shown that these scores match real skill or that using the app changes behaviour at work.
  • One scoring flaw is known. The exact-code trap can be credited only if you use a keyword weight, which is unfair to a reasonable design that doesn't.

11. Glossary

TermMeaning
SBERTA type of model that turns sentences into numbers, so that text with similar meaning can be found.
EmbeddingThose numbers: a list that represents the meaning of some text.
EngineThe search method: Lexical (keywords), Static (a small embedding model) or MiniLM (a small transformer).
ChunkingSplitting long text into smaller pieces before searching it.
nDCG@5How good the top five results are, rewarding the best ones appearing first. 1.0 is perfect.
Recall@5The share of relevant reviews found in the top five.
Cost unitsA count of the work a design needs. It's the same on every device.
Held-out queriesTest queries kept out of the Lab, used to check a design after you submit it.
Static hostA service that delivers website files and runs no code of its own.
TrapA problem built into the challenge on purpose, to see whether you notice it.
